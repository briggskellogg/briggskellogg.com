On Being Booed

may 2026

This month, at two separate university commencement ceremonies, the mention of artificial intelligence was met with booing.1 Not polite disagreement. Booing. From graduates. At their own ceremonies. In the middle of speeches meant to send them off into the world.

I work in the industry being booed at.2 I want to say some things about it plainly, because plainness is what the moment requires, and plainness is the one thing the industry I work in is currently refusing to offer.

The students are not wrong to boo.

I begin there because most of what has been written about this in the last week has been written by people whose first instinct was to explain the students to themselves — to suggest they don't understand the technology, or that they've been misled by alarmism, or that they'll come around once they see the productivity gains.3 This is the voice of an industry that has decided the people most affected by its product are the people least qualified to have feelings about it. It is the voice you use when you have something to hide and you're hoping the listener won't notice you're hiding it.

The students notice. They are twenty-two years old, not stupid. They have spent four years and a hundred thousand dollars preparing for a labor market in which only thirty percent of last year's graduates secured full-time work in their field, down from forty-one percent the year before.4 They are correct to be afraid. They are correct to be angry. They are correct that the people on the stage telling them everything will be fine are mostly the people who stand to profit from everything not being fine for them in particular.5

Booing is, under the circumstances, a remarkably restrained response.

The sin of my industry right now is not the technology. The technology is what it is. Some of it will be good, some of it will be ruinous, most of it will be ambiguous in ways we won't understand for a decade. That's true of every technology. That's not the problem.

The sin is the vagueness.

Every public-facing sentence about what this technology will do to working people passes through a filter designed to make it impossible to act on. “Augmenting human capability.” “Reshaping the nature of work.” “Creating new opportunities we can't yet imagine.”6 Each of these phrases is doing the same job: replacing a question someone could answer with a fog no one can argue with. The question is: will my job exist in five years? The fog is: the future of work will be transformed.

I have sat in rooms where the honest answer was known. I have watched the honest answer get sanded down into the fog before it left the room. I have done some of the sanding myself.7 This is not a confession that requires absolution — I'm not the villain of the story and neither are my colleagues, most of whom are decent people doing what they understand to be reasonable work. But the cumulative effect of thousands of decent people each sanding down their own small piece is a public conversation in which the people who know the most are telling the people who need to know the most the least useful version of the truth.

The students are booing the fog. They are right to.

I am not going to tell you what to do, graduate, because I do not know what you should do. Anyone telling you what to do right now is either selling something or lying.8 The honest position is that the next five years are going to be strange and that nobody — not the people building the technology, not the people regulating it, not the people writing op-eds about it — knows exactly how strange.

But I can offer you one thing, because I have asked it of myself.

Assume the parts of your education designed to make you a competent processor of information are now worth less than they were when you started school. Assume the parts designed to make you a person — judgment, taste, the ability to read a room, the ability to know when something is wrong even though everyone around you is saying it's fine, the ability to care about a specific other human being and act on that care — are worth more.9 The machines are good at the first and not good at the second, and the evidence that they'll become good at the second is thinner than the people selling them want you to believe.

Do not retreat into nihilism, because nihilism is what the people in charge are hoping you'll do. A demoralized graduating class is a graduating class that doesn't organize, doesn't vote, doesn't demand things, doesn't notice when its government quietly stops representing it. The fog is downstream of an interest in your not noticing. Stay noticing.

And don't let anyone — including me — convince you that being scared is unreasonable. It is reasonable. The fear is information. The fear is, in this case, a more accurate reading of the situation than the optimism being offered to you. Trust the fear. Then do what humans have always done with fear: use it to figure out what to do next, slowly, in the company of people who love you.

There is a thing the industry I work in tells itself, late at night, when the question of what it is doing comes up. The thing is: someone is going to build this technology. If we don't build it, someone worse will build it. Therefore the people who care should build it, so that at least the people in the room when decisions are made are the people who care.

I have made this argument. I have heard it made. It is not a stupid argument. It is also the argument that every powerful institution in history has made about its own continuation, and the historical record on whether the people in the room actually behave like they care once they're in the room is mixed at best.10

What I would ask of the people in the room — the colleagues I work with, the leaders above me, the founders whose names are on the buildings — is that we stop hiding behind the fog. The fog protects us, not the public. It lets us avoid saying out loud what we know to be true about the next five years. It lets us collect our salaries and tell ourselves we are the good guys because we have not personally fired anyone. The fog is the moral hazard. The fog is what the students are booing.

Speak plainly. Tell people what is coming. Tell them what you don't know. Tell them where the technology helps and where it harms and which of those two outcomes you are working on this week. If you cannot do this in public because your company will fire you, do it in private with the people in your life who need to hear it. Tell your nieces. Tell your nephews. Tell the kid from your hometown who's about to take out loans for a degree in a field that may not exist. Tell your own children. Tell them what you actually think.

The people booing at commencement this month are not asking us to stop building. They are asking us to stop lying. That request is so much smaller than the panic-readers of this moment are making it out to be. It is also so much more important.

I write this from inside. I am not quitting my job. I am not announcing a moral conversion. I have a family to take care of and I believe, on balance, that the work I am doing inside this industry is more useful than the work I could do outside it. These are choices I have made and I will live with them.

But I will not pretend the students are wrong. They are not wrong. They have seen the fog and named it, in the only language they had available to them on a Saturday morning under a tent, which was booing. They are owed a better response than condescension. They are owed plainness.

At the University of Central Florida on May 10, real estate executive Gloria Caulfield called AI “the next industrial revolution” and was booed by graduating arts and humanities students; she paused, looked at the other speakers, and asked, “What happened?” Days later, at the University of Arizona, former Google CEO Eric Schmidt was booed multiple times during his commencement address as he likened the emergence of AI to the rise of the personal computer. At one point Schmidt said, “If you'd let me make this point, please —” while the crowd jeered. Schmidt's invitation had been protested by student groups before the ceremony even began. Two incidents in two weeks at two different schools is not yet a pattern. It is, however, no longer an outlier.↩
