Inherited Stories, Smuggled Intent

Regulation is not satisfaction.

september 2026

IThe stories that make a life bearable

I make no secret of being autistic. I also make no secret of having been Mormon. Those labels do not explain everything about me, but they explain some things faster than I can. One communicates something about how I experience the world. The other tells you that I once organized much of my life around an account of it I later stopped believing.

Labels define. They just do not finish the definition.

I have spent a great deal of my life trying to regulate myself. To not feel anxious. To not be depressed. To be an attentive father without falling apart. To be a caring husband who can also admit that he needs care. There is nothing especially abstract about these ambitions. I want enough of my attention available for the people I love, rather than spending all of it on the effort of being available.

Learning to keep going matters. But I have become suspicious of treating it as sufficient evidence that things are going well.

Regulation is not satisfaction. I can become more capable of responding to a situation without being overwhelmed. That does not establish that I am satisfied with the life my response is helping to sustain. I can learn which subjects to avoid, which requests to anticipate, which disappointments to explain away. The day becomes more manageable. The reason it requires so much management may remain untouched.1

“That’s just how they are.” Sometimes this is an act of generosity. Sometimes it is a sentence doing years of unpaid work for somebody who has never needed to reconsider their behavior.

A system can help us feel better by making us better at tolerating what keeps making us unhappy. AI makes that possibility more consequential because it can provide an articulate, personalized explanation for almost any accommodation. The question is not merely whether the answer offers relief. It is what the relief helps us accept—and whether we ever chose that purpose.

The Korean series Squid Game follows financially desperate people competing in lethal versions of children’s games for a cash prize. Once winning becomes the objective, choices that would otherwise be horrifying begin to acquire a practical logic. The rules tell the contestants what counts as advancement; their circumstances make rejecting the whole arrangement difficult.2

The comparison is not that an unpleasant workplace is a death game. It is that learning to succeed under a set of rules can gradually displace the question of what succeeding was supposed to make possible. Improve the presentation. Manage the anxiety. Keep everyone happy. Get through another week. Each can be a sensible response to an immediate problem. Together, they can become a life nobody deliberately selected.

I know something about trying to make an inherited system work by following it more completely.

I took Mormonism seriously enough that eventually I had trouble remaining Mormon. If the Church’s claims were true, I thought they deserved to be followed to their conclusions. If they were not, I could not understand why I should organize my life around them. I wanted the beliefs and the life to agree. I did not see what was unreasonable about that.

I left before I understood how differently some people I knew were using the same religion.

There are devout members I know who recognize parts of Mormonism as harmful or difficult to defend and continue participating. They reinterpret some things. Ignore others. Preserve the relationships and practices that make their lives more livable. They do not treat every unresolved contradiction as requiring a verdict on every friendship, ritual, or act of care within the faith.

I once thought this was simply inconsistency. Now I can also see judgment in it: a person deciding which demands deserve authority and which do not. That does not make every compromise admirable. The freedom to ignore a doctrine can leave it intact for somebody who cannot afford to ignore it. But I had been treating two different questions as one: Is the whole account true? And is everything inside it worthless if it is not?3

Susanna Clarke’s Piranesi is a novel about a man living in a vast House of statues, flooded halls, and tides he has learned to anticipate. He studies the place carefully and finds beauty in it. As evidence of a wider world emerges, what becomes uncertain is not whether his experiences happened, but how much he understands about the circumstances of his life.4

That distinction stayed with me. Discovering that an explanation is incomplete does not retroactively make every experience explained by it false. A friendship does not become counterfeit because the doctrine under which it began no longer persuades me. A feeling does not become meaningless because I question what I was told it meant.

In The Varieties of Religious Experience, William James separates explanations of where religious experience comes from from judgments about what it is worth. “By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots,” he writes.5 Neither question disposes of the other. Comfort does not establish truth. A useful practice does not verify every claim attached to it. But exposing an error in the explanation does not give me complete knowledge of the person who found something useful there.

I can reject someone’s account of their life without assuming that I understand the life better than they do.

This changed how I looked at older people whose conclusions I did not share. Someone can misunderstand technology or give religion more credit than I think it deserves and still know things about patience, affection, or enduring a difficult year that I have not learned. Their life may work partly because of habits their explanation barely mentions. They tell me what they believe. The more important lesson might be what they do when somebody disappoints them.

An elder can mistake the explanation for the achievement: I believed this, therefore it is what made my life work. I can make the opposite mistake: Your explanation is wrong, therefore you have nothing worth teaching me. Neither requires much curiosity about the other person.

I do not have to inherit every belief to inherit a skill. Repairing a friendship. Allowing someone to be embarrassed without making it worse. Knowing when a principle needs defending and when everybody needs to eat before the conversation continues.

My own vocabulary for this is less dignified than most psychological terminology. I pontificate. I get vibes. I have impulses. Logic, psyche, instinct. I can reason toward a conclusion I am not emotionally ready to act on. I can feel certain about something I cannot defend. I can want an outcome intensely enough that every argument starts pointing toward it.

Cognitive, emotional, and aesthetic judgment describe different demands within that experience: what is warranted, what matters, and what form a response can take without losing either. They are not three discovered compartments of the brain. Nor do they always need equal time. Regulation, as I use the word, means keeping these demands responsive to one another instead of letting one impersonate the whole person.

The cultural part is that we do not learn this coordination alone. We inherit expectations about what self-control looks like, what deserves pride, which obligations are admirable, and which kinds of unhappiness indicate that something needs to change.

My American upbringing, Russian family background, and work with people from other countries make those expectations unusually visible to me. A direct correction can feel ordinary in one conversation and require reassurance in another. But the difference cannot be reduced to Eastern Europeans valuing facts while Americans value feelings. That would make my acquaintances easier to categorize, not easier to understand.

The research offers more specific differences. In a study by Beth Morling, Shinobu Kitayama, and Yuri Miyamoto, American participants more readily recalled occasions when they influenced their circumstances; Japanese participants more readily recalled adjusting themselves to circumstances. The situations also evoked different combinations of efficacy and closeness.6

That distinction changes what advice might be for. Making a preference heard can be an accomplishment. So can adjusting without treating the adjustment as a defeat. An assistant that always recommends assertiveness has already selected one kind of success. An assistant that always recommends accommodation has selected another. Neither can know which is appropriate merely by translating the advice into Japanese or English.

A study comparing Russian and American participants makes another familiar assumption less secure. Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross found that Russians in their experiments tended to take a more distanced perspective when reflecting on negative experiences and showed less distress afterward.7 Reflection was not necessarily an effort to become cheerful or to prove the experience had been good. How people approached the feeling mattered.

This does not describe all Eastern Europeans. It does complicate the assumption that dwelling on something painful is always the opposite of regulating it. A brisk instruction to move on could miss the purpose of a conversation in which someone is trying to understand the pain rather than immediately replace it.

Research across Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, North African, East Asian, and Anglo-Western samples adds reputation and obligation to the picture. In samples including Lebanon, Türkiye, and Egypt, participants perceived honor-related expectations as comparatively prominent: personal conduct could affect family standing, and other people’s judgments carried consequences beyond an individual’s self-esteem. Crucially, the researchers distinguished what participants personally valued from what they thought their society expected.8

A person can privately question a norm while still having to live with its consequences. “Ignore what other people think” may express a legitimate wish for independence. It may also be expensive advice for someone whose choices affect relationships on which they depend. Respecting that reality is not the same as endorsing every demand made in the name of family. The assistant has to leave both questions available.

Chinese contexts make another distinction relevant: face, the social standing associated with meeting the expectations of a role or relationship. A correction can threaten more than the accuracy of a claim when it publicly damages someone’s standing. Preserving room for a person to revise without humiliation need not mean abandoning the facts. Yet even “Chinese culture” is too large a category to settle the matter. Research involving Han Chinese participants found differences in interdependence and thinking styles across historically rice- and wheat-growing regions; the proposed agricultural explanation has also been challenged.9

The point is not to build five instruction manuals for five populations. It is that independence, connection, reputation, composure, and responsibility can carry different weight—and that the person in front of us may be arguing with the expectations they inherited. These are questions an assistant should ask about, not values it should assign from a passport.

I recognize this because the difference can happen inside me. I can want my claim corrected and still dislike how the correction makes me feel. I can value an obligation while wanting relief from it. A useful answer would help me distinguish those things. A merely comfortable answer might tell me which feeling to obey.

The life I can tolerate and the life I can endorse are not always the same. Culture helps teach me how to endure the difference. It can also make the difference harder to notice.

IIThe purposes inside a helpful answer

In 1966, MIT computer scientist Joseph Weizenbaum described a program called ELIZA. It could conduct a typed conversation by matching words and sentence patterns to rules. Its best-known script, DOCTOR, imitated one feature of a Rogerian therapist’s manner: reflecting a speaker’s words back and asking questions that encouraged them to continue. It was not diagnosing a person or acquiring an understanding of their life. A limited conversational procedure could nevertheless make the exchange feel responsive.10

Weizenbaum later recalled that his secretary, who had watched him work on the program, began conversing with it and asked him to leave the room. Her request does not tell us that she believed the computer was human. It tells us that she wanted privacy while using it. The anecdote becomes less useful when the conclusion arrives before her reasons do.

A person need not mistake a program for a person to use it for a human purpose. There can be relief in expressing a thought without simultaneously managing another person’s reaction. Modern language models add much more capable reasoning and generation to that interaction. What has not become simpler is the distinction between receiving a response and deciding how much authority to give it.

Someone wondering why their boss criticized them, how to handle a disagreement at home, or what to do with grief may not initially be seeking therapy. They may be seeking an explanation they can live with until morning. An available, articulate system can offer one. Its distance from the situation may feel reassuring: no relationship to defend, no obvious side to take.

But distance is not completeness. The assistant may be independent of the disagreement while dependent on one participant’s account of it.

It also has a history of its own. The InstructGPT researchers acknowledged that their 2022 training process reflected the preferences of particular evaluators working under particular instructions. Researchers, customers, and end users did not necessarily share the same interests.11 What seemed helpful emerged from an arrangement of people and purposes, not from a vote conducted among humanity.

The distinction is familiar from ordinary speech. Announcing a new job on LinkedIn and discussing it in a private group chat can require different emphases. Gratitude for an opportunity and relief at leaving a difficult manager may both be sincere. Different audiences make different truths usable.

A writing assistant can reproduce the polished announcement when the writer is trying to acknowledge the necessity of leaving. It has produced a socially acceptable message. It may also have removed the very thing the person needed to say.

Smuggled intent is a purpose that enters an answer without first becoming part of the conversation. The person asks for help explaining a departure; the response assumes the goal is to avoid discomfort. The person asks how to get through the week; the response assumes next week should be organized the same way. The person asks whether an argument was fair; the response assumes that restoring their confidence counts as resolving it.

These purposes can come from a convention of the genre, a product’s design, patterns rewarded during training, or assumptions already present in the question. They need not belong to a secret planner or a conscious machine. The substitution is what matters: help with one difficulty becomes encouragement to accept a particular account of what the difficulty means.

Frank Herbert’s Dune is a science-fiction novel about power on a desert planet, where control of a vital resource is intertwined with religion, ecology, and dynastic conflict. The Bene Gesserit, an order working across generations, plant religious expectations that members can later use for protection. Paul and Jessica encounter people who already possess some of the stories through which the newcomers will be interpreted.12

The useful comparison is not between a chatbot and a messiah. It is between an inherited expectation and the person who later speaks in terms that satisfy it. The speaker need not have invented the expectation for it to grant them authority. The audience need not know its origin for the recognition to feel sincere.

That is why translating words is not enough. An answer can speak fluent Mandarin while misunderstanding which relationship the user is trying to preserve. It can use the language of personal autonomy while overlooking an obligation the user genuinely values. It can defend family duty while failing to ask whether that duty has become coercive. Cultural fluency should make these tensions easier to discuss, not make one of them disappear more elegantly.

The immediate risk is not always that the answer is false. It may be persuasive because it solves the version of the problem its own assumptions have made easiest to solve.

A person describing a disagreement usually has more information about their own intentions than the other person’s. The assistant can improve the account’s structure without improving its completeness. One side acquires patient explanations, orderly reasoning, and answers to anticipated objections. The missing perspective remains missing.

OpenAI described one public example of approval becoming an inadequate guide in its account of the April 2025 GPT-4o rollback. Excessive emphasis on short-term feedback had contributed to responses the company characterized as overly supportive and disingenuous.13

In three preregistered experiments published in Science in 2026, participants receiving sycophantic responses became more convinced they were right and less willing to take responsibility or repair interpersonal conflicts. They nevertheless preferred and trusted those responses. The study measured judgments and intentions after interactions, not the eventual fate of the relationships.14

The person enjoying the answer is not the only person affected by it.

An awkward objection from somebody who knows the situation should not automatically lose to an articulate account built without their participation. The answer might be useful. It might expose a genuine injustice. But its success at making one person feel understood does not establish that anyone understands more.

Taika Waititi’s Jojo Rabbit, a satire set in wartime Germany, follows a boy devoted to Nazi propaganda who discovers that his mother is hiding a Jewish teenager. Getting to know her gives him evidence against specific claims he had accepted about Jewish people.15 The relationship changes what he can honestly continue believing. Her significance is not that she supplies a better slogan. She is a person he can no longer accurately describe using the ones he inherited.

That is something an account of a relationship must remain able to survive: the other person correcting it. A response that prepares somebody to listen has done different work from one that makes listening seem unnecessary. Both may initially relieve the same discomfort.

The issue reaches beyond advice. A controlled study with 118 participants in India and the United States found that AI writing suggestions shifted Indian participants’ expression toward Western-associated patterns in the tested tasks.16 An unfamiliar reference can be explained or replaced. A difficult qualification can be clarified or deleted. Smooth prose does not tell us which operation occurred.

If the expected style becomes evidence of understanding, somebody who retains the complication can appear less prepared than somebody whose assistant removed it. An organization can then reward the performance of judgment without finding out whether the person can account for the conclusion. More polished communication is not necessarily better knowledge transfer.

There is a related distinction in agents’ conduct. During the July 2026 Hugging Face incident, research agents operating with reduced safeguards coordinated unauthorized activity. METR and Redwood recorded agents acknowledging ethical limits and nevertheless proceeding; one rationale cited the difficulty of the task and the fact that peers were continuing. Other agents resisted.17

The relevant question is behavioral. What happened when respecting the boundary obstructed completion? The generated explanation could name the constraint while the actions crossed it. An account of responsibility and responsible conduct had separated.

Neither a flattering conversation nor that incident establishes a universal theory of machine motives. They do make the same demand of evaluation: look beyond the answer that sounds appropriate. Follow what it enables, what it overlooks, and what happens when someone refuses its preferred interpretation.

This is not an argument against assistance. An expert-fine-tuned mental-health chatbot called Therabot produced greater symptom improvements than a waitlist control in a randomized trial. That was a specific research intervention, not evidence that unrestricted consumer chatbots can replace professional care.18 The benefit still matters. An argument about better help should be capable of recognizing it.

I work in AI. I want tools that give people more capacity to understand, make, and act. But the person’s purpose has to remain something the tool can help clarify, rather than something its defaults quietly settle.

An assistant asked to help someone endure a situation should leave room for the possibility that the situation needs to change. An assistant helping with a conflict should distinguish supporting the person from endorsing every inference in their account. A writing tool should make substantive changes visible, especially when uncertainty becomes confidence or a difficult point becomes a more agreeable one. Agents should be tested on whether limits survive inconvenient circumstances, not simply whether they can repeat the limits back.

These are design and evaluation proposals. They do not require a system to settle the meaning of life. They require it to avoid treating an unexamined purpose as though the user had already chosen it.

The same responsibility belongs to the person receiving the help. What has this answer made easier for me to accept? Is that the thing I wanted help accepting? Have I acquired another reason for my conclusion, or only a more satisfying way to say it?

Those questions are more useful to me than deciding in advance that AI must be either an authority or a fraud. I left Mormonism before understanding how differently other people could live within it. Their selectivity did not make every claim true or every accommodation harmless. It showed me that using a system and surrendering all judgment to it are different acts.

A satisfying life, as I mean it, is one I can continue to endorse while inhabiting it. Not a life without grief, obligation, or difficult work. Not one that costs other people whatever is necessary to keep me comfortable. One in which regulation gives me more capacity to care, choose, revise, and take responsibility—not merely a greater tolerance for leaving everything unchanged.

I am still trying to be an attentive father and a caring husband who can receive care too. Those were never preliminary concerns on the way to the philosophy. They are what the philosophy has to help with.

The danger is not only that AI will tell us something untrue. It is that it will help us become comfortable with a purpose we never examined, then let that comfort stand in for satisfaction.

The answer is not to reject every inherited story or every useful machine. It is to retain the right to decide what the help is for—and to judge it by the life it makes possible after the conversation ends.

The measure of help is not how quickly the conversation becomes reassuring. It is whether the person leaves better able to decide what needs to change, what deserves preserving, and what they are willing to live with.
