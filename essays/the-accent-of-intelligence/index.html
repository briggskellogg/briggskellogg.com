The Accent of Intelligence

Who teaches a machine what a good answer sounds like?

september 2026

IBetter

Someone has to decide which answer is better.

A question. Several possible responses. A person paid to put them in order. In one influential approach to training language models, comparisons like these helped teach a system which answers to reward. The human judgment became part of the machinery.1

That sounds sensible. It is sensible. We would generally prefer that someone examine the answers before the answers become a product.

But better has arrived without its paperwork.

One response is correct but abrupt. Another is patient and wrong. A third takes four paragraphs to avoid admitting that it does not know. Which one wins? What does the evaluator think the question is asking? What have they been instructed to value? What would they choose if this were a conversation rather than a job?

The difference interests me more than the vocabulary. Although the vocabulary is where I started.

There is a tempting explanation for why AI writes the way it does: the people helping train it have left their accents behind. A word that sounds peculiar to an American reader might be ordinary somewhere else. The machine, despite its expensive presentation, might still carry evidence of the people whose labor made it presentable.

In the familiar case of delve, the explanation that traces its overuse to Nigerian English has not been established. Research comparing varieties of English did not support that account. Later work offered evidence that learning from human feedback can contribute to word overuse, without settling why the evaluators preferred those words.2

The uncertainty leaves us with a more interesting question.

Perhaps what survives is not simply how someone speaks. Perhaps it is how they believe they should speak. How they were taught a competent person sounds. What they think the assignment wants from them.

Those are different inheritances.

I have written before about being a human whose communication was read as artificial. I do not intend to spend the rest of my life adding preventable confusion to a sentence so that it will pass as mine.3 But the experience makes me suspicious of a standard of naturalness that does not know it is a standard.

An accent is easiest to hear in someone else.

IIThe instructions

In January 2023, TIME reported that Kenyan workers employed by Sama on an OpenAI project received roughly $1.32–$2 an hour in take-home pay. Sama disputed the range. The work involved labeling harmful material, not establishing a demonstrated origin for the model’s favorite words.4

The distinction matters. A wage does not, by itself, explain an adjective. Being poorly paid does not make someone’s judgment poor. And noticing labor only when we dislike the product is a peculiar form of concern for the people doing it.

The question is what authority accompanies the work.

A person can help produce a standard without being allowed to determine its purpose. They can disagree with an instruction and competently follow it. They can recognize distinctions the available choices do not preserve. None of this requires us to imagine what a particular worker secretly thought. It requires us to stop treating a submitted answer as a complete account of the person who submitted it.

The researchers behind InstructGPT were explicit about this. Their 2022 paper describes preferences shaped by instructions and the conditions of paid work. It distinguishes the contributions of labelers, researchers, and customers. It also notes that customers and end users need not be the same people, or have the same interests.5

The paper asks whose preferences the model is being aligned to. That question should survive contact with the marketing department.

Human feedback is a useful technical description. It is a very incomplete description of a human arrangement. It tells us that people participated. It does not, by itself, tell us who could change the instructions, which disagreements mattered, or who would live with the result.

Suppose two evaluators disagree about reassurance. One thinks a worried person needs immediate comfort. The other thinks comfort would be dishonest before the facts are known. A ranking must eventually be recorded. But the disagreement may contain information that the winning answer cannot carry by itself.6

I am not arguing that every disagreement must remain unresolved. A factual error does not acquire equal standing because someone sincerely prefers it. I am asking whether we have distinguished an error from a different account of what help requires.

We should welcome human judgment in these systems. We should also be interested in the circumstances under which it was obtained.

An accent can survive the process while the reason for it disappears.

IIIThe correction

Imagine that a colleague has made a consequential mistake. A number in a document is wrong. Someone will act on it unless it changes.

You can identify the number and give the correct one. That is part of the work.

You also have to decide whether to interrupt the meeting, send a message, or speak afterward. You need to judge whether your colleague understands the problem or is still defending against the embarrassment. You may need to be blunt. You may need to stop enjoying how clearly you can demonstrate that you are right.

There is no sentence that settles all of this in advance.

A good correction requires a relationship between capacities. Reason checks the claim. Concern attends to what the correction will do to the people involved. A sense of form tells you whether this much explanation belongs here, whether the joke helps, whether another sentence would clarify the point or merely prolong your performance.

I would call that last capacity aesthetic judgment.7 It is the sense that something fits—or that something technically competent is wrong for the occasion. It operates when a sentence needs cutting and when a silence needs leaving alone.

These capacities can disagree. Your wish to avoid hurting someone can conceal information they need. Your certainty can outrun your evidence. Your pleasure in a clean explanation can make the remaining complication feel like an editorial problem.

The complication may be the person.

This is what I mean by regulation: the ongoing adjustment of one demand in the presence of another. Not permanent calm. Not three immaculate faculties taking turns. The work includes noticing that the thing you are good at is, in this moment, making you worse at something else.

Language participates in that work. It does not exhaust it. Research distinguishing language from other cognitive capacities gives us reason not to treat verbal fluency as a complete inventory of a mind. It does not give us permission to declare that machines cannot think, or that every convincing sentence is a fraud.8

The more useful question is what the sentence remains answerable to.

Culture matters here because we learn expectations about how an interaction should feel. Even the emotional state regarded as desirable is not universal. Affect-valuation research distinguishes how people feel from how they want to feel, and documents cultural variation in those ideals. Excitement and calm do not occupy identical positions in every account of a good life.9

So consider a system instructed to sound supportive. Does support mean lifting the person’s mood? Helping them tolerate an unpleasant fact? Taking their side? Giving them enough room to discover that there are sides they have not considered?

A warm response can do any of those things. Its warmth does not tell us which one it did.

And this is where a preference for how intelligence sounds can become a much larger preference about what intelligence is for. If we reward the appearance of a well-conducted interaction, we still have to ask what happened to the interaction.

The correction is not finished when it sounds good.

IVThe return

In a controlled experiment reported at CHI in 2025, researchers gave 118 participants in India and the United States culturally grounded writing tasks, with or without AI suggestions. In that setting, the assistance shifted Indian participants’ writing toward styles associated with the American comparison group.10

The paper includes two descriptions of biryani. These are different writers in different conditions, not the before-and-after drafts of one person. The unassisted example names Malabar preparation, nutmeg, and accompaniments including dates chutney. The assisted example emphasizes “rich flavors and spices” and meat that “melts in my mouth.” It still names Hyderabadi biryani.11

General praise makes a description familiar. An unfamiliar detail gives a reader somewhere to go. Neither is always preferable. But recognizing a subject and discovering something about it are different encounters.

Now imagine asking for help with a passage you have written. You may mean: make this easier for someone else to understand. You may mean: help me sound appropriate to this audience. You may mean: I cannot yet express the thought clearly enough to find out whether I believe it.

All are reasonable requests. They authorize different changes.

Removing a grammatical error is not the same decision as making a speaker sound more certain. Replacing an unfamiliar reference is not the same decision as explaining it. Smoothing a hesitation can be useful. It can also remove the only visible evidence that the writer has not settled the question.

The danger is not that revision happens. The danger is that several kinds of revision can arrive under one reassuring description: improvement.

There is a related problem in conversation. In three preregistered experiments involving 2,405 participants, Myra Cheng and colleagues found that sycophantic AI responses increased people’s conviction that they were right while reducing their stated willingness to take responsibility and repair interpersonal conflicts. Participants nevertheless preferred and trusted those responses.12

The study did not establish permanent changes to personality. It established something narrower, and already worth our attention: being pleased by an answer and being helped by it can come apart.

A service evaluated only at the moment of satisfaction would struggle to distinguish them. The apology not attempted would happen after the favorable rating. So would the conversation not reopened. The relevant person may not even be the person using the product.

This is why I resist using emotional intelligence as a synonym for producing agreeable emotional language. Emotional judgment has to include what the reassurance makes possible afterward. Sometimes comfort is exactly what is needed. Sometimes a person needs a question the comfortable answer has allowed them to avoid.13

There is a further possibility here, which is my inference rather than another result from those experiments. Imagine two people on opposite sides of an argument receiving the same habitual affirmation. They need not leave with identical beliefs. They could leave more certain of incompatible ones.

The common product would be a manner of being right.

That is a cultural possibility worth worrying about: a shared standard of response that makes people less able to respond to one another. No universal opinion is required. The agreement is between each person and the way the system treats them.

VStill online

This is the version of the dead internet that interests me.

Not a census of bots. Not a claim that everyone except the author has stopped thinking. An internet can contain actual people and still make their encounters narrower than they need to be.

Consider the prospect of a communication environment in which more people can produce a polished contribution, but more of those contributions have passed through the same judgments about what deserves emphasis, what sounds excessive, and what counts as complete. The activity could increase. So could satisfaction. Neither would, by itself, tell us how much room remained for an unfamiliar judgment.

There is already evidence that individual gains and collective narrowing can coexist. In a 2024 short-story experiment, Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser found that access to AI-generated ideas improved evaluations of individual stories, especially for participants with lower baseline creativity scores. The stories also became more similar to one another.14

The benefits are part of the problem the essay has to solve. They cannot be waved away because they complicate the warning.

A person who can finally express something has not committed a cultural offense by accepting help. Neither has a person who chooses a conventional register because the unconventional one keeps getting them excluded. I will not tell someone to remain illegible so that the rest of us can enjoy the diversity.

And difference alone is a poor standard. An error can be distinctive. Cruelty can be authentic. A familiar explanation can open a subject that an idiosyncratic one made impossible to understand. We should want to change some of the things that make us recognizable.

The question is whether the change is available to judgment.

Can the person understand what the assistance has done? Can they refuse its standard without losing access to the assistance? Can the result introduce something the system was not already inclined to reward?

These questions leave room for AI-assisted work to become culture, rather than treating it as a contaminant. They also demand more than an assurance that a human clicked the button.

My concern is a culture that keeps the opportunity to publish while gradually weakening the opportunity to answer differently. That is a risk, not a completed diagnosis of the internet.15 The studies above concern different systems and different tasks. They do not add up to a single experiment in the death of civilization, however convenient that would be for the title.

The internet does not have to go silent for us to have less to say to one another.

VIShow me

A person is allowed to change.16

That includes changing because of a machine. Learning a new register, encountering a better argument, discovering a form that makes an inarticulate feeling usable. Assistance that never alters anything would be an expensive form of storage.

I work in AI. The assistance in this essay reaches into its sentences.17 I want tools that make more expression possible. I am not asking for a return to a world in which technical fluency determines who gets to make the thing they can imagine.

But I want the nature of the help to be visible.

An editor could distinguish correcting an error from adapting a passage to an audience, and both from changing the writer’s position. It could identify where uncertainty became confidence. When it replaces a specific reference with something more familiar, it could make that decision available for inspection instead of treating familiarity as a neutral improvement.

Those are design proposals. They would have to be tested. A screen full of explanations can become another thing the user is expected to approve without reading.18

Evaluation would have to change too. Alongside whether people preferred the answer, we could ask whether they retained the relevant meaning, recognized the limits of the claim, or became better able to handle the next interaction. Different tasks would need different measures. The point is to follow the help far enough to see whom it helped.

And the evaluator should not disappear from the discussion once the feedback has been collected. The ability to identify a badly framed task, register a meaningful disagreement, or question an instruction is part of the quality of the judgment we are asking a person to supply. A more geographically varied workforce cannot, by itself, resolve the authority of the same unchanged rubric.

None of this gives us a system without an accent. I do not think that is a coherent ambition. Even the choice to sound neutral expresses a preference about what neutrality should sound like.

It gives us a different ambition: help that can explain its interventions well enough for another judgment to remain possible.

Return to the person comparing answers. Which is better? We now have reasons to ask what the answer preserved, what it made easier, and what happened after someone accepted it. There may still be a clear winner. The question has become more demanding, not unusable.

I do not need intelligence to sound like me. I need to be able to notice when sounding like it has changed what I mean.

Show me what you changed.
