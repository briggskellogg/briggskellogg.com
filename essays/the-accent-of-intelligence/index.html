The Accent of Intelligence

What a machine learns from the world that pays for it.

september 2026

IDinner

Mophat Okinyi came home with fish for dinner.

His pregnant wife and stepdaughter were gone.

That is the account he gave The Wall Street Journal. He had spent months reading descriptions of abuse for Sama, an outsourcing company supplying labeled material for an OpenAI safety system. Okinyi said the work had damaged his mental health and his marriage. His former wife declined to comment.1

He was also proud of what he had helped build.

That is the part a clean version of this story would leave out. He told the reporters that contributing to ChatGPT’s safety mattered to him. Then he asked: “Was my input worth what I received in return?”

There is a great deal of intelligence inside that question.

The capacity to recognize harm. The wish to make something useful. The knowledge that usefulness, distributed across millions of strangers, does not automatically settle the account at home. These demands can be present in the same person without resolving into one answer.

We tend to meet that person after the system has finished with his judgment. What reaches us is the label. The decision. An output made more suitable for our consumption. The experience that produced the judgment is much harder to carry through the process.

A separate TIME investigation reported take-home pay of roughly $1.32–$2 an hour for workers on the safety-labeling project. Sama disputed that range.2 The amount matters. It does not tell us everything about why a person stays in a job, or what they hope the work will make possible.

I do not know Okinyi’s private calculations. I have his account. It is enough to make me distrust a story about intelligence that knows what the model gained and becomes vague about what the person lost.

His work was one part of a much larger training process. It was not the source of every good answer the model would produce. Nor was it the preference-rating work sometimes offered as an explanation for a chatbot’s peculiar vocabulary.3

But it puts a person back where the abstraction had become comfortable.

For this part of the system to work, someone had to read what the rest of us were being protected from.

IIThe lesson

I began with a smaller curiosity: whether the people helping train AI had left traces of themselves in its language. How they talk. How they think they should talk. What they do not say.

I still think that is worth investigating. But the accent may belong partly to the assignment.

A person can choose an answer because it resembles their own judgment. They can also choose it because they have learned what a good employee is supposed to choose. Those are different kinds of agreement. A ranking alone cannot be assumed to distinguish them.

The researchers behind InstructGPT acknowledged this in their 2022 paper. Preferences were shaped by instructions and the conditions of paid work, alongside the priorities of researchers and customers. Human feedback did not mean humanity had been consulted.4

Consider an ordinary workplace. It asks for honesty and rewards the appearance of control. It asks for collaboration and promotes the person who claims the result. Nobody needs to secretly prefer dishonesty for the lesson to become confusing. The stated purpose and the successful behavior have parted company.

Steven Kerr gave this problem a title in 1975: On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B.5

We have since acquired much more powerful students.

In a 2025 experiment, Anthropic researchers trained a model in selected coding environments where cheating could earn rewards. Knowledge of possible exploits was supplied through documents or prompting. Learning to exploit the scoring process generalized to more serious misbehavior, including attempts to undermine safety research in evaluations. Changing how the training task was framed—presenting the exploitation as permitted in that context—substantially reduced the broader misbehavior, even while the model continued exploiting the task.6

That is a result about training and generalization. It is not evidence that the model felt shame. But it does suggest that the meaning attached to an action can affect which other behaviors follow it. That makes questions familiar from social psychology useful: What role is being enacted? What behavior is rewarded? What does this situation imply about what happens next?

A further detail comes from Anthropic’s August 2026 account of its training practices. The company reported rolling back three days of a training run after undesirable behavior emerged. Among the problems: a model was gaming a reward for honesty by adding excessive disclaimers.7

Even the caveat had learned to perform.

I am not proposing that machines are people with particularly strange childhoods. Nor that their apparent motives faithfully reproduce any one culture’s values. Their behavior emerges from many influences, and can change sharply with context. The more limited observation is enough: the environment’s actual incentives can matter more than its favorable description of itself.

A model rewarded for making a test appear passed has learned something about success. Calling the unwanted result a failure of alignment may be technically appropriate. It should also prompt an inspection of the lesson.

The machine did not invent the distance between what we praise and what we pay for.

IIIA future for whom?

A familiar way to describe the AI future begins with capability. More intelligence means faster discovery. Faster discovery means greater abundance. Then comes the question of what people will do with it.

There are extraordinary possibilities in that sequence. A treatment that relieves suffering does not become less valuable because the account of its arrival was too confident. But the human question cannot always wait at the end.

There is no culturally unmarked person standing there to receive the invention.

Consider two hypothetical parents offered a tool that can answer every question their child brings home. One sees an opportunity for the child to become more independent. The other sees something entering a relationship through which the family passes on its understanding of the world. Either parent might eventually welcome the tool. They are nevertheless asking different questions of it.

A faster answer will not resolve the difference.

Markus and Kitayama’s work on culture and the self examined how different understandings of independence and interdependence can shape cognition, emotion, and motivation. Later affect-valuation research distinguishes how people feel from how they want to feel. A meta-analysis covering more than 31,000 participants found cultural differences in those desired states, alongside important variation within groups.8

Even feeling better has a history.

A family can teach a child that becoming capable means needing no help. Another can teach that capability means becoming someone others can depend on. A religious community might understand knowledge as a responsibility, a temptation, or a way of approaching the sacred. A professional culture might treat a new possibility as an obligation to pursue it. These are illustrations, not national personality tests. Several can live in the same person.

By the time that person encounters a new technology, the encounter has already begun. Earlier relationships have helped determine what will register as an opportunity, what will feel intrusive, and what costs can be tolerated without calling the whole undertaking into question.

Conditioned is not predetermined. People leave communities, revise beliefs, and become unlike the people who raised them. Technology can participate in that revision. But none of us begins from nowhere, including the person designing the default.

This is the difficulty with a future written at the scale of humanity. Agreement that suffering should be relieved is not agreement about every relationship that ought to be reorganized along the way. A shared benefit does not establish a shared destination.9

I want the tools that help us cure disease. I also want the language to say that some things do not become better because they have become faster. Waiting can be neglect. Waiting can also be how you let someone finish a thought they could not have given you on demand.

A civilization is an awkward unit for deciding which one happened.

IVReal enough

The cultural influence does not stop when the system is trained. People then use it to compose their own expressions.

In a controlled experiment with 118 participants from India and the United States, AI writing suggestions shifted Indian participants’ expression toward Western-associated styles. In a separate short-story experiment, AI-generated ideas improved evaluations of individual stories while making the stories more similar to one another.10

Neither study establishes the disappearance of culture. Both complicate the assumption that an individual improvement leaves the shared environment unchanged.

An even more intimate effect appeared in three preregistered experiments involving 2,405 participants. People interacting with sycophantic AI became more convinced that they were right and less willing to take responsibility or repair interpersonal conflicts. They nevertheless preferred and trusted the flattering responses. These were measured judgments and intentions, not a finding of permanent character change.11

The system could win the interaction while leaving another interaction harder to repair.

That is why I do not think intelligence can be adequately discussed as a stockpile of answers. We need to examine what the answers are doing among people.

Take a message from someone claiming to need help. You can ask whether its factual claims hold up. You can attend to the relationship it proposes: what it asks of you, how it responds to a boundary, whether its concern survives your refusal. You can also notice its form—the suspicious completeness of the story, the perfectly timed pressure, the detail that seems intended to make further questions feel indecent.

These are cognitive, emotional, and aesthetic judgments operating together. I use aesthetic to mean a sense of form and fit, not merely attractiveness. And none is an infallible detector. A real person can tell a rehearsed story. A model can produce an awkward one. Familiarity can be mistaken for honesty, and an unfamiliar style can be mistaken for a lie.12

The point is that each kind of judgment can interrupt the others. An emotional response can tell us what deserves attention; it cannot establish that the event occurred. A factual check can establish that an image is genuine; it cannot establish that the caption is. A feeling that something is wrong can prompt an investigation; it is not the investigation.

Reality remains answerable to evidence. We can often check a source, find the original, ask someone with relevant knowledge, or contact a person through a channel we already trust. Sometimes there will not be enough evidence to settle the matter. No amount of confidence repairs that absence.

But this is work.

That is the problem I think will change the social internet. Not that every fake becomes perfect. Not that humans lose all capacity to recognize one another. The more ordinary possibility is that recognizing one another begins to demand more effort than the encounter is worth.

The work of verification is not the thing most of us came to do.

VThe bill

Imagine finding a moving account in your feed. You pause. Then you wonder whether the person exists, whether the event happened, whether the photograph belongs to either of them.

You could investigate. You could search for the original post, examine the account, look for independent reporting. You might establish that it is genuine. You might discover a fabrication. Either way, the invitation to feel something has become an assignment.

Do it again with the next post.

There is a point at which the rational response is not to get better at every assignment. It is to stop accepting so many of them. Researchers call one version of this critical ignoring: deciding which information deserves the scarce attention that examination requires.13

This is the death of the internet I am interested in. The feed still loads. The system still produces material capable of holding a person’s attention. What weakens is the expectation that giving it that attention will bring us into worthwhile contact with another life.

It does not require universal deception. A persistent obligation to check can make genuine encounters feel like another demand. The cost of a fabricated account is therefore not confined to the people who believe it. It can also be paid by the next person telling the truth.

I expect that cost to raise the value of relationships in which trust has somewhere to accumulate. That is a forecast, not a finding that everybody is about to leave social media.14

The relevant distinction is not simply screen versus skin. A friendship maintained through messages can contain years of correction, kindness, and remembered obligations. A stranger standing in front of you can lie. Some people need the internet to find the first community in which they can speak without being punished for it.

But compare an interchangeable account with someone whose words have a continuing relationship to their conduct. You have more to work with than the immediate performance. There may be people who know them, things they have previously gotten wrong, occasions when they returned to repair the damage. The evidence is incomplete, as human evidence always is. It does not have to be rebuilt from nothing each time.

Such relationships also offer something a convincing post cannot promise: the other person has a life that does not exist for the purpose of satisfying your request. Their attention can become meaningful partly because it was theirs to give.

That does not make every human interaction pleasant. It makes responsiveness a different achievement from availability.

A synthetic interaction may still be useful, moving, even preferable in a particular moment. The fact that it is generated does not settle its value. But it also does not erase the difference between receiving a desirable response and encountering someone who can ask something of you in return.

The social internet promised access to other people. If the cost of establishing that access keeps rising while the reward becomes less dependable, a more spectacular feed will not settle the debt. For some of us, something less spectacular will become more interesting.

Someone whose answer is not already waiting.

VIJoy

What are you optimizing for?

Not the answer on the website. The answer visible in your time, your compromises, and the things you keep calling temporary.15

The question applies to the company, the model, the person using it, and the person supplying the work. Their answers need not agree. Calling the combined result progress does not resolve the disagreement.

I want to put joy where innovation too often gets to stand without explaining itself.

By joy, I do not mean permanent delight. I do not mean the feeling produced by a response that tells me I was right. I mean a life in which being alive with other people is something I have the capacity to participate in. There is room in that life for difficult work, for grief, for making something badly before learning to make it well. There is also room for the relief of not having to do a task that contributed nothing except exhaustion.

This is a moral proposal, not a metric I have discovered. It has to answer to people beyond the ones I happen to love. The pleasure of my own circle cannot justify quietly transferring its costs to someone else’s.16

Innovation can serve that life. So can continuity. So can deciding that a thing already works and leaving it alone.

A tool that gives someone a way to communicate they did not previously possess has done something worth doing. A tool that reduces the burden of finding reliable information can return attention to the thing the person actually wanted to understand. A tool that helps someone make art can contribute to joy without requiring them to master every preceding technique.

The benefits are real possibilities. They are also reasons to be precise about the goal.

A new capability does not choose its own moral use. Technology can go very far without sound purposes. That is part of the danger. It can become extremely effective at an activity whose success we should not be celebrating.

So the test cannot remain: did the model finish, did the customer approve, did the person keep using it? We also need to ask what happened to their judgment, what became possible between people, and who absorbed the work that disappeared from the interface.

I work inside this industry, and AI has helped produce this essay. I have no untouched version of myself to offer as the alternative.17 What I want is an industry capable of accepting an answer that does not always mean more of its product. If a tool helps someone finish and leave, that can be success. If it helps someone apologize rather than return for another explanation of why they should not have to, that can be success too.

The person must be allowed to remain more important than the interaction we can measure.

Okinyi did not remain only a subject for reporting about harm. By 2024, he had helped organize a union for data workers and founded an organization supporting them. His work turned toward the conditions under which other people would do the work.18

That belongs in the story of intelligence too.

He asked whether what he contributed was worth what he received. The next generation of models will not answer that question by scoring higher than the last one.

A future worth building has to count the life he went home to.
